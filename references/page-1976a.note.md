# Page 1976 (Paper I) — verification note

**Citation.** D. N. Page, "Particle emission rates from a black hole: Massless particles from an uncharged, nonrotating hole", Phys. Rev. D 13, 198 (1976); received 18 August 1975. Read in full from the scanned PDF supplied on 18 October 2026 (pp. 198–206), using OCR plus the page images. OCR garbles exponents, so the figures below were checked against the rendered pages and the paper's own unit conversions.

**Not the paper B3 cites.** B3 cites Page's Paper II, "… Massless particles from a rotating hole", Phys. Rev. D 14, 3260 (1976), for spin-down before mass loss. This is Paper I, which treats only a non-rotating hole. On rotation it says only that emission at maximal rotation decreases a* = J/M², and that it was "not yet known" whether a* keeps decreasing near zero (§I). Paper II is still needed for B3's spin-down claim.

**What it supports elsewhere.**
- **B10, pair emission threshold.** "A black hole with M > 10¹⁷ g would emit virtually no known massive particles". Electrons and positrons are emitted ultrarelativistically for roughly 4.5 × 10¹⁴ g ≪ M ≪ 9.4 × 10¹⁶ g (eq. 24). Holes with M ≲ 5 × 10¹⁴ g emit muons and heavier particles. This supports "electron–positron pairs from black holes below about 10¹⁴ kg, and hadrons in the final stage".
- **B4, lifetime and power.** For M ≫ 10¹⁷ g: P = 2.011 × 10⁻⁴ ħc⁶G⁻²M⁻² = 3.458 × 10⁴⁶ (M/g)⁻² erg s⁻¹, split 81% neutrinos, 17% photons, 2% gravitons. Lifetime τ ≈ 2.16 × 10⁶⁶ (M/M☉)³ yr (eq. 27). For one solar mass that is about 8.6 × 10⁻²⁸ W, a mass loss of about 1 × 10⁻⁴⁴ kg s⁻¹.
- **Primordial black holes.** One decays within the present age of the universe if its initial mass is below (5 ± 1) × 10¹⁴ g, about 5 × 10¹¹ kg.
- **Superradiance.** For bosons in the superradiant regime ω < mΩ, "waves are amplified rather than absorbed" (§II).

**Caveat on numbers.** Page 1976 treats neutrinos as massless, so they carry 81% of the power. With massive neutrinos, which are not emitted by large holes, only photons and gravitons remain. The power is then about five times lower and the lifetime about five times longer. That brings a solar-mass lifetime to order 10⁶⁷ years and the mass loss to order 10⁻⁴⁵ kg s⁻¹, B4's present figures. If B4 cites Page 1976 for the numbers, it should say they rest on the massless-neutrino assumption, or cite a modern update.

**Status.** Read 18 October 2026. Supports B10's thresholds and, with the caveat, B4's orders of magnitude. Paper II is still needed for B3.
