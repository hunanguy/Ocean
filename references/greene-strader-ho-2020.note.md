**What the paper gives.**
1. Of ten galaxies within 4 Mpc with stellar masses 10⁹–10¹⁰ M☉ and dynamical constraints, five have detected central black holes (M32, NGC 5102, NGC 5206, NGC 205, NGC 4395) and five have only upper limits (NGC 300, NGC 7793, NGC 404, the LMC, M33): occupation fraction > 50% (p. 16; p. 41).
2. M33: upper limit < 1500 M☉ on any central black hole (Gebhardt et al. 2001), which "would suggest that the occupation fraction is not unity" for galaxies near 10⁹ M☉ stellar mass, if taken at face value (p. 41).
3. X-ray surveys give an occupation fraction of at least 20%, likely ~70%, in 10⁹–10¹⁰ M☉ galaxies; tidal-disruption rates suggest it is roughly flat from LMC to Milky Way mass (p. 41).
4. The black-hole-mass–velocity-dispersion relation (M_BH–σ*) continues unbroken down to M_BH ~ 10⁵ M☉ and σ* below 100 km/s, with large scatter at the low end, attributed partly to a broad range of black-hole masses (abstract; pp. 37, 46).
5. Late-type (disk) galaxies have a lower normalization in M_BH vs stellar mass than early types; this has been read as black-hole mass not correlating with disk mass (p. 38).
6. Nuclear star clusters and black holes coexist often at low galaxy mass; high-mass galaxies have black holes but few nuclear star clusters (p. 38).

**Bearing on the Ocean.** Keith's picture: the swirl's speed sets its tightness at the center, and tightness decides whether a black hole forms. The data show no sharp threshold in galaxy mass, but a fall in occupation toward low mass with some galaxies having none (M33) and others small ones (NGC 4395), and a black-hole mass that tracks the central speed spread σ* tightly and steeply, while tracking disk mass poorly. So the speed that matters is the central one (σ*), not the outer rotation.
